## The question

I build reporting for regulated environments — federal programmes now, life
sciences before that. One question keeps coming back and I have never had a
clean answer to it: when a system hands an analyst a queue of records to review,
how do you justify the order?

"The model ranked them" does not survive contact with an auditor. Neither does a
score between nought and one that nobody has checked. I could not publish the
work where this actually came up, so I rebuilt the problem on open data: could I
build a compliance review queue over hospital admissions, and then **prove** the
ordering was better than chance?

## The data, and what was wrong with it

55,500 admissions from a public dataset. Two problems, one small and one that
changed the project.

The small one was ordinary mess, and the dbt tests now catch it: 108 rows with
negative billing down to −$2,008, 534 exact duplicate rows, and inconsistent name
casing across 98% of records.

The large one was mine. I had treated the data as real, and left a comment in the
loader saying so. It is not — it is generated, and the tell was in the dashboard I
built on top of it. Billing is flat against every cut: correlation with length of
stay is **r = −0.006**, the spread across five insurance providers is 0.9%, and
elective, urgent and emergency admissions cost the same to within $105.

Real hospital billing never looks like that. Emergency costs more than elective;
longer stays cost more than short ones. Flatness that uniform across every
dimension is the fingerprint of independently sampled columns.

That constrained what the project could claim, and I would rather state the
constraint than quietly work around it: **no cross-sectional finding from this
data is real.** The pipeline is the artefact under review. The numbers about
healthcare are not findings.

## What I did, and the call I would defend

The first version scored each record with an IsolationForest and rank-normalised
the output to a tidy nought-to-one "risk score."

That number was uniform by construction. Its mean was 0.49999 and its 95th
percentile 0.94998 — which is what a uniform distribution looks like, and not
evidence of anything. It had the shape of a risk score and carried no
information. There was no way to tell whether a record scoring 0.94 deserved
review.

So I manufactured ground truth. A module appends copies of real admissions,
mutated into five failure modes a revenue-integrity review would actually flag —
a discharge before the admission, a stay past 400 days, a charge 12–30× the
median for that condition, a large negative charge, a one-day elective stay
billed like a major episode — at a 1% contamination rate, labelled. The detector
never sees the labels. It fits on the contaminated data, ranks everything, and
the ranking is scored afterwards.

**The call I would defend:** this measures whether the detector surfaces the
failure modes *I thought of*. It says nothing about the ones I did not. That is a
real limit and it is why I report the per-mode breakdown rather than the headline
alone — the average hides exactly the case that matters.

## What changed

<StatRow from="headline" />

The benchmark's first job was to score the pipeline as it already stood. It came
back at average precision 0.319. Two changes took it to 0.887.

<Bars from="configurations" max={1} height={210} />

Neither change was clever. IsolationForest picks its split feature uniformly at
random, so eleven one-hot indicator columns sitting beside four informative ones
meant most splits carried no signal. And scikit-learn defaults `max_samples` to
256 — at a 1% contamination rate that subsample almost never contains an anomaly,
so moderate outliers sit inside the sampled range and take as long to isolate as
ordinary records.

The more useful result is what refused to move.

<Bars from="failureModes" max={1} names={{ value: "After", baseline: "Before" }} />

Four failure modes go to 0.99 or above. The fifth — a discharge recorded before
the admission — sits at 0.604 under every configuration I tried.

The reason is not tuning. **A negative length of stay is invalid, not unusual.** A
stay of −12 days sits comfortably inside the numeric spread of ordinary stays, so
a detector that measures distance-from-typical scores it as unremarkable. No
amount of tuning fixes a model that is answering the wrong question.

It became a dbt test. Rules enforce validity; the model handles what is merely
strange. Splitting those two is the thing I would carry into the next pipeline.

## What I would do differently

**I would write the benchmark first.** I built a scorer, shipped it, and only
later discovered its output was noise — weeks of work resting on a number nobody
had checked, including me. Building the benchmark took an afternoon. Everything
interesting in this project came out of it.

**I would inject harder anomalies.** Mine are separable enough that four of five
modes reach 1.000, which flatters the detector more than it deserves. A charge
sitting just outside a condition's normal band, rather than twelve times its
median, would be a far more honest test — and I would expect the numbers to drop.
