## The question

A pipeline that ranks records for review is only half a system. Someone still has
to open the queue, decide what to look at, and later explain to somebody senior
why they looked at that and not something else.

In the reporting I do for federal and life-sciences clients, the question that
ends every dashboard review is the same one: *where does this number come from?*
Not "is the chart pretty" — where does it come from, who defined it, and what
happens to it between the source system and the tile on screen. I have watched
that question stall a release more than once.

So the brief here was two things at once. Build the dashboard a compliance
reviewer would actually work from, and build the artefact that answers the
question about every measure on it.

## The data, and what was wrong with it

The dashboard is what caught it, and it caught it in the least ambiguous way I
have seen.

Five insurers by six conditions is thirty independent cells. Every one lands
within about 5% of the same figure, and the column totals are tighter still.
The bar chart above it — elective, urgent and emergency — is three bars of
visually identical height.

Widen it out and nothing moves anywhere:

Real hospital billing does not behave like this. An emergency admission costs
more than an elective one. A three-week stay costs more than an overnight. Payer
mix moves the average. Flatness this uniform across every dimension at once has
one ordinary explanation: the columns were sampled independently of each other.

That reframed the project. **No cross-sectional finding from this data is real** —
not one. The pipeline and the documentation are the artefacts worth reviewing;
the healthcare conclusions are not conclusions. I would rather say that at the top
of a README than have a reviewer work it out and wonder what else I had not
checked.

## What I did, and the call I would defend

The deliverable I care about here is not the `.pbix`. It is
[`docs/METRICS.md`](https://github.com/ManishDhawal/Healthcare_Risk_Compliance_Pipeline/blob/main/docs/METRICS.md):
every published measure, its definition, and its lineage from source column
through the dbt staging view to the tile. It lives in the repo beside the code so
a definition and its implementation change in the same commit.

It records the choices that are invisible on screen and contentious in a room:

- **Billing is attributed to the admission month, not the discharge month.** An
  episode spanning a month boundary lands entirely where it started. Apportioning
  across days is equally defensible; the dashboard picks one and says so.
- **`avg_los_days` excludes rows with a null length of stay. `admissions` does
  not.** So the volume count and the denominator behind the average differ. That
  is deliberate — a missing discharge date should not delete an admission from
  the volume — but it is exactly the discrepancy someone finds in month three and
  escalates.
- **`risk_score` is a ranking, not a probability.** A 0.65 does not mean 65%
  likely to be a problem.

**The call I would defend** is the smallest one on the page: the average-billing
axis starts at zero. It produces three bars of the same height and a chart that
appears to say nothing. Truncating that axis to a $24k–$26k window would have
produced three visibly different bars and a story about elective admissions —
a story that is an artefact of the axis, not the data. The chart is supposed to
report that there is no difference. It does.

## What changed

The dashboard's most useful output was a negative one, and it redirected the
whole project: the modelling work moved to detecting records that are internally
inconsistent — an impossible stay, a charge that does not match its episode —
rather than relationships between columns that were never there.

It also turned up three defects in itself, which is the other reason to build the
thing before writing about it:

**It displayed patient names.** First column, on the page a reviewer spends most
of their time on, in a project branded risk and compliance. Synthetic names, but
the practice is what gets judged. Identifiers now come out at the model layer,
not by hiding a visual.

**The scatter plots sums.** Billing against length of stay, both aggregated by
risk tier — five points. That chart shows how big each tier is, not how the two
measures relate. To show a relationship you plot records, or bins.

**One measure does not add up.** "High risk by insurer" puts Aetna near fifty
where the other four sit between two and three thousand. Across five roughly
equal groups scored on a distribution that was uniform by construction, those
bars should all be about the same height. I do not yet know whether it is the
slicer, a relationship, or the measure — but an earlier version of this project's
README read that bar chart as a *finding* about payer concentration. It was not
a finding. It was a bug I had not looked at hard enough.

## What I would do differently

**Write the metric dictionary before the first visual, not after.** Every
definitional question above was decided while dragging fields onto a canvas and
reconstructed later. Deciding first would have taken an hour and produced the
same dashboard with fewer things to explain.

**Drop identifiers at the model layer from the start.** Removing a column from a
visual is not the same as it not being in the model, and I had done the easy one.

**And name the fields for people.** The axes still read `insurance_provider` and
`admission_type` — database column names, on a page built for someone who has
never seen the database.
