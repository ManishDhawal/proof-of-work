## The question

This was a team capstone: a dual-head DenseNet doing bone-fracture detection and
15-class chest pathology from the same backbone. I owned the data side — the
evaluation, the feature engineering, and the question of whether the training set
was fit to learn from in the first place.

That question turned out to be the whole project. The fracture half worked almost
immediately. The chest half never did, and the reason was in the label
distribution rather than the architecture.

## The data, and what was wrong with it

112,120 scans from NIH ChestX-ray14, multi-label, with 801 distinct label
combinations. The distribution is not skewed so much as it is two different
datasets stacked on top of each other.

<Bars from="distribution" />

More than half of every batch is "No Finding". Hernia has 227 examples in the
entire corpus — a 266-to-1 ratio against the majority, and 0.20% of the data. On
a random 70/15/15 split that leaves roughly 34 Hernia scans in validation.

There is a second problem underneath that one, and it is the more serious of the
two. **The labels are not ground truth.** ChestX-ray14's labels were mined from
free-text radiology reports with NLP, and the dataset's own documentation puts
that extraction well short of perfect. So the rarest classes are simultaneously
the least represented and the most likely to be mislabelled. Anything the model
learns about Hernia, it learns from a couple of hundred examples that may not all
be Hernia.

## What I did, and the call I would defend

We attacked the imbalance in three places at once, none of them the loss function
alone:

- **Batch composition.** A generator built every batch as 50% fracture and 50%
  chest data, and down-weighted "No Finding" sampling within the chest half. The
  model never saw the raw 54% majority.
- **Split logic.** Stratified on the fracture label, and separated "No Finding"
  from findings when splitting the chest set, so validation could not drift to a
  different mix than training.
- **Focal loss** (α = 0.25, γ = 2.0) on both heads, so easy negatives stopped
  dominating the gradient.
- **Per-class decision thresholds**, tuned down as class frequency fell — 0.50
  for "No Finding", 0.05 for Hernia.

We also wrote an augmentation utility — 90° rotation, horizontal and vertical
flips, a 1.5× contrast multiply, a 5×5 Gaussian blur — meant to widen the rare
classes by simulating scanner variation.

**The call I would defend is that none of this was unreasonable, and I would
still reach for most of it.** Batch-level rebalancing is a better instrument than
synthetic duplication when the minority class has 227 real examples, because
copies of 227 images do not contain more information about Hernia than the 227
originals do. The thing I would defend less is the fourth item, and the reason is
the next section.

## What changed

The model reported recall of 1.000 on nine of fifteen conditions. On a problem
this imbalanced that should have been the first thing we distrusted, and it was
not.

Recall near 1.0 with precision near 0.02 is not a model that has learned a rare
condition. It is a model answering **yes** to nearly every scan for that class.
Perfect recall is trivially achievable by predicting everything positive; it only
means something alongside the precision it cost.

The mirror image confirms it. "No Finding" — the majority class, 53.8% of the
data — finished with **recall 0.0015 and precision 1.0000**. The model had almost
entirely stopped predicting the most common label in the dataset, and was right
the handful of times it did.

Put those two facts together and the diagnosis is not "the rare classes are hard".
It is that **we over-corrected.** Down-weighted majority sampling, plus focal
loss, plus thresholds that fell to 0.05 for the rarest classes — three
interventions all pushing the same direction, compounding into a classifier whose
answer was "abnormal" regardless of input. Best F1 across all fifteen conditions
was 0.331, on Infiltration. Most sat below 0.10.

And the augmentation utility, which was the one intervention that might have
pushed the other way? **As far as the committed code shows, it was written and
never wired into the training loop.** It is defined in the preprocessing module
and called nowhere in it, nor in the model or application code. The project plan
lists augmentation as done. The code does not support that.

For contrast, the fracture head — 4,606 versus 4,640 examples, essentially
balanced, single-label — reached validation AUC 0.9982 and F1 0.9775 without any
of this machinery. Same backbone, same training loop, same week. The difference
was never the model.

## What I would do differently

**Watch precision-recall, not recall.** One metric moving toward 1.0 while its
partner collapses is a signature, and we had the numbers on screen for ten epochs
without reading them that way. Average precision per class would have made the
degeneracy obvious in epoch two.

**Change one thing at a time.** Sampling weights, focal loss, and per-class
thresholds were introduced together, so when the model tipped into
predict-everything there was no way to attribute it. Any one of them alone is
defensible; the interaction is what broke it.

**Verify the intervention actually runs.** Writing `augment_array` and assuming
it was in the pipeline is a failure of exactly the kind this project was supposed
to catch. A single assertion on batch size, or one saved sample image, would have
shown it.

**And I would question the labels before the architecture.** With 227 examples of
uncertain provenance, no amount of sampling strategy makes Hernia learnable.
The honest recommendation was to drop the rarest classes, or reframe the task as
detecting the eight conditions with enough clean support — not to keep tuning
against labels that could not carry the weight.
